# Link Analysis Guidelines

The method behind the graph — source evaluation, the association matrix, the link chart and the questions of association analysis — rewritten from the UNODC [*Criminal Intelligence: Manual for Analysts*](https://www.unodc.org/documents/organized-crime/Law-Enforcement/Criminal_Intelligence_for_Analysts.pdf), with a final section on how each step maps onto Quaesitor.

## From Information to Intelligence
The manual opens with three definitions (chapter 1, p. 1). *Information* is "knowledge in raw form". *Intelligence* is information "that has been evaluated in context to its source and reliability", information with added value. *Analysis* is the separating of a thing into its parts and the tracing of things to their source to find the principles behind them. The whole discipline fits in one line from the same page:

> INFORMATION + EVALUATION = INTELLIGENCE

Analysis goes beyond the facts. It tells you how good or poor your information is, what you did not know before, what you still need to know, where to look next, and how to explain your understanding to others (p. 2).

The analytical process is a loop, not a line (chapter 5, figure 5-1, p. 29): collate and sift the available information, draw a **preliminary link diagram**, develop preliminary inferences, write a focused collection plan, collect more, evaluate the new data against the old, revise the inferences, and only then draw conclusions and write the report. Every hypothesis should answer who, what, how, where, why and when (p. 31). Two of the manual's "ten standards for analysts" (p. 33) matter most here: the minimum analytical product is a **written report**, and a chart is acceptable only as an addition to it, never instead of it; and compiling data without comparison or thought is not analysis.

## Evaluate Before You Link
A link chart is only as good as the grading of the information behind each line. Chapter 4 describes the **4 x 4 system** used by Europol and widely adopted by police agencies (pp. 25-26). Three principles govern it: evaluation is professional judgement, not feeling; the source and the information are graded **separately**; and the grading is done as close to the source as possible.

| Source | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **A** | No doubt about authenticity, trustworthiness, competence; a history of complete reliability |
| **B** | A source whose information has in most instances proved reliable |
| **C** | A source whose information has in most instances proved unreliable |
| **X** | Reliability cannot be judged |

| Information | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1** | No doubt about accuracy |
| **2** | Known personally to the source but not to the officer passing it on; logical in itself; agrees with other information |
| **3** | Not known personally to the source, but corroborated by information already recorded |
| **4** | Not known personally to the source and cannot be corroborated |

The two grades travel together as a code such as `B2`. Some agencies add a **handling code** (who may receive the report: own agency only, national agencies, international agencies, with conditions), giving codes like `B24` (p. 28). A 6 x 6 variant with finer grades (A to F, 1 to 6) exists and converts easily (pp. 26-27). After grading, reports are **sanitised** so that nothing in the wording reveals the source (p. 27).

For link analysis the rule is simple (p. 38): a link is **confirmed** when its information is graded A1, A2, B1 or B2. Any other grade makes it **unconfirmed**. This one distinction drives the whole drawing convention below.

## What Link Analysis Is
The manual's definition (p. 35):

> "Link analysis puts information about the relationships among entities—individuals, organizations, locations, and so on—into a graphic format and context that will clarify relationships and aid in inference development."

The raw material of an investigation is voluminous and scattered across reports, records and statements. The analyst's basic problem is putting it together in an organised way so that meaning can be extracted. Link analysis does that for one kind of meaning: who is connected to whom, through what, and how strongly.

A network has four features (pp. 45-46):
- **Entities:** the items under investigation. People, businesses, organisations, vehicles, locations, events, objects, phones, accounts.
- **Relationships:** family ties, reciprocity (exchange and compromise), suitability (the right person for the job), bonding (past associations), control (hierarchy or threat), dominance, subordination, succession.
- **Directionality:** the flow of information, favours and authority. It shows how the network works inside.
- **Strength:** a judgement, based on the interactions recorded and on the grading of the data.

Two warnings from the same pages. A chart "is not in itself an analytical product, it is an analytical tool": producing it is a prerequisite of analysis, not the analysis. And a chart is a freeze-frame of the group at one moment; often the useful question is how the group changed over time.

## The Seven Steps
The manual describes the method by hand (pp. 35-41) and notes that software simplifies the mechanics but not the thinking.

1.  **Assemble all raw data.** Files, field reports, informant reports, records, open sources.
2.  **Determine the focus of the chart.** Read everything and mark the entities the chart is about: names of people and organisations, plates, addresses, numbers. A chart about everything shows nothing.
3.  **Construct an association matrix.** A triangular grid with the entities on the diagonal. The matrix is a working step, never a presentation product, and it is built *before* any chart. Order the entities to make checking easy: individuals alphabetically, then organisations alphabetically, then plates and addresses in alphanumeric order.
4.  **Code the associations.** In each cell, a symbol for the kind and grade of the link. The manual suggests six codes (figure 6-4, p. 38): confirmed association; suspected association; confirmed membership of the organisation (officer, manager, employee, club member); suspected membership; confirmed investment with no other participation (shareholder, limited partner), with its direction from owner to owned; suspected investment. Any consistent set of symbols works, as long as the chart carries a key.
5.  **Count the links of each entity.** Across and down for every row. The counts tell you where to start drawing: the entities with most links go near the centre.
6.  **Draw a preliminary chart.** Every association in the matrix becomes a line between two symbols.
7.  **Clarify and re-plot.** Long and crossing lines confuse the reader. Redraw until the picture is clear, uncluttered and concise. Every chart carries the time, the date, a sequence number and a key, so that older and newer versions can be told apart, which matters for disclosure (p. 40).

An example of steps 3 and 4 with invented data: three people and two organisations, `●` confirmed association, `○` suspected association, `+` confirmed member or officer, `-` suspected member, `→` confirmed shareholding.

| | Bianchi | Conti | Rossi | Delta Srl | Club Orione |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Bianchi** | | | | | |
| **Conti** | ● | | | | |
| **Rossi** | ○ | ● | | | |
| **Delta Srl** | + | → | | | |
| **Club Orione** | | + | - | | |

Step 5 gives Bianchi 3, Conti 4, Rossi 3, Delta Srl 2, Club Orione 2. Conti goes in the middle.

## Drawing Conventions
The conventions in chapter 6 (pp. 39-45) are few and worth keeping exactly, because every reader of a chart expects them:
- **Shapes:** circles for individuals, rectangles for organisations, businesses and other collective entities.
- **Lines:** a solid line for a confirmed link, a dotted or dashed line for a suspected one.
- **Ownership:** a percentage on a solid line; the line points from owner to owned.
- **Membership:** a person who is a member or officer of an organisation is drawn inside its rectangle, with the position written next to the name when it is known (chairman, director, secretary).
- **Implied links:** two people inside the same organisation have an implied link through their roles. Draw a solid line between them only if information supports it; otherwise, if your analysis gives reason to, draw a hypothesised line and say so in the key.
- **Unknowns:** an unknown person is still an entity. Draw the circle, label it "unknown", and keep the link; the gap is information.
- **Company trees:** drawn like a family tree from parent to subsidiaries, with the registration number under each name and any known criminal involvement noted on the box.

The manual's eleven layout examples (figures 6-12 to 6-22) cover the recurring shapes: one person in two companies with another officer in each; one person in three companies alone; three people in one company with and without positions shown; an outsider linked to a company but not to its officers; an outsider with a confirmed link to the company and a suspected link to one officer; two companies associated with no known link between their officers; a person linked to an unknown; a person who is an officer of a chain of subsidiaries; a holding with many subsidiaries; the same holding drawn as a tree with registration numbers and a fraud case noted. When your case has one of these shapes, draw it the way the manual does and the reader will recognise it.

## Reading the Chart: Association Analysis
Drawing is the prerequisite; the analysis is in the questions you then put to the chart. The manual lists thirty-one "linkage issues" (pp. 46-47), grouped here:
- **Structure:** Who is central? What is the hierarchy? Are some members connected to each other to the exclusion of the rest? Which three people, if removed, would sever the network? Who could take over the roles of the key people? Does the structure make the group vulnerable to infiltration?
- **Flows:** Who initiates the interactions? How often do they happen? What is exchanged? Which communication links are most worth monitoring? What do the financial links say about the operation?
- **Bridges and roles:** Who forms the liaison between distinct organisations? What role does a given individual appear to play? Which names look like aliases of the same person?
- **Context:** What business links, links to other criminal activities, links to places ("the territory") and links to regulatory or government structures exist? What is known of the leader's way of running things and of the group's propensity to violence?
- **Time:** Have the links changed? Are they changing in strength or centrality? What earlier bonding elements are known?
- **Comparison:** Can this model of linkages be applied to other groups? Have similar structures been seen before, here or elsewhere? Does this structure help predict the next one?

The written product of association analysis has a fixed shape (p. 47): an executive summary of the findings (the bottom line, written first for the reader and last by the analyst: see [Report Writing](field-notes/report-writing.md)); an overview of the group answering the relevant questions above; the chart or charts; a biographical summary of each target and potential target; conclusions about the group; recommendations for tactical or strategic action, with the list of **unanswered questions** and where the missing information might be found (the intelligence gaps).

Expanded into a process (p. 48), the twelve steps are: collect; organise and collate; extract the association material; prepare the matrix; prepare the chart; write the biographical summaries; summarise the chart; apply the questions above to the network; establish what is present and what is absent; draw interim hypotheses; list the unanswered questions and the collection and investigative steps to answer them; present the findings and the written report.

## Pitfalls the Manual Warns About
- **A finished-looking chart is the most dangerous:** it is a tool, and it ages. Date it, number it, and redraw it as the information changes.
- **Form must never beat substance** (p. 32): a clean chart of ungraded information is a clean picture of guesses.
- **Mirror imaging** (p. 31): projecting your own reasoning onto the people on the chart. Their logic is theirs.
- **Unanimity:** "When everyone agrees on an issue, something probably is wrong" (p. 32).
- **Excess:** excessive detail hides the important facts. The client wants to know what the intelligence means, not how much you collected.
- **Hearsay drawn as fact:** a line drawn solid on a `C3` is a false statement on a chart that may be disclosed.

## Doing It in Quaesitor
Quaesitor automates the mechanics of steps 3 to 7 and leaves the thinking where it belongs. How each element of the method maps onto the program:

| Method | In Quaesitor |
| :--- | :--- |
| Entities | Nodes. Persons, phones, e-mails, accounts, places, vehicles, documents and the investigative and Follow the Money types in the catalogue ([ontology.md](ontology.md)). Identity rules merge two entries of the same entity into one node, which is what the matrix diagonal does by hand. |
| Focus of the chart | Mark the subject as **target**; use category filters and **saved views** to keep one chart per question. |
| Association matrix | The program keeps it for you: every link is a cell. The summary of connections in a node's card is the row count of step 5. |
| Confirmed and suspected links | Link **style**: solid for confirmed, dashed for suspected. Put the 4 x 4 grade (`A1`, `B2`, `C3`) in the link label or in a field of the card, so the grade travels with the line. |
| Direction | The **arrow** on the link, set by clicking the preview. Owner to owned, payer to payee, superior to subordinate. |
| Ownership with percentage, positions, membership | **Follow the Money relations** on the link: Ownership with `percentage`, Directorship with `role`, Membership, Employment, Family, Payment with amount and date. The label is generated as `verb (caption)`. |
| Person inside an organisation | A Directorship, Employment or Membership link to the organisation node, with the role; the hierarchical layout draws the organisation above its officers. |
| Unknown person | A person node labelled "unknown" with the link; replace the label when the name arrives, the identity stays. |
| Hypothesised link | A dashed link with a label starting with "hypothesis" and the reasoning in the link's notes. |
| Time, date, sequence number, key | A saved view per revision, named with date and number; the PDF export of the graph carries the date. The key is the legend of styles you adopt in the case notes. |
| Freeze-frame versus evolution | Several saved views over time; typed relations carry `startDate` and `endDate`. |
| Grades and sanitisation | Source grades in the card fields; the raw report in **document sources**, indexed and searchable, never on the chart. |
| Biographical summaries | The node card: notes, grouped connections, raw records, images, PDF export of the card. |
| The written report | Outside the program. Export the graph and the cards to PDF as its attachments; the report remains the product. |
| Collection plan and gaps | A text node per open question, linked to the entities it concerns, until it is answered and deleted. |

Two things the program does not do, by design. It does not grade information for you: the grade is a judgement, and the manual's three principles apply to the analyst, not to the software. And it does not write the report: the standard says a chart is an addition to a written product, and that remains true with a better chart.
